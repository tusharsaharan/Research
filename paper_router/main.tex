\title{Certified on Average, Not per Language:\\
Language-Conditional Risk Control for Cheap-vs-Premium LLM Routing}
\author{Tushar Saharan \and Sahil \and Shivam\\ IIIT Allahabad}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
LLM routers send easy queries to a cheap model and hard ones to a premium model.
Recent work certifies such routers with distribution-free guarantees, but the guarantee
is \emph{marginal}: it bounds quality loss averaged over all users. We ask whether it
holds for code-mixed (romanized Hindi--English, ``Hinglish'') users separately.
On 307 GSM8K problems asked in both English and Hinglish, the cheap model's
unrecoverable-error rate is 28.0\% for Hinglish vs 11.7\% for English on the \emph{same}
problems (exact McNemar $p<10^{-6}$). A single certified threshold violates the guarantee
for one language group at every risk level, in up to 64\% of calibration/test re-splits
(allowed: 10\%): English at $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.10\}$, Hinglish at $\alpha=0.20$.
Uncalibrated and conformal-risk-control thresholds exceed the budget for their worst group
in 43--95\% of re-splits. Certifying each language separately with Learn-then-Test keeps every
violation rate $\le$ 5.8\% and, at $\alpha=0.10$, saves 53\% (English) and 20\% (Hinglish)
of premium cost. Results hold on a larger unpaired set with ARC questions added. We compare against uncalibrated thresholds,
conformal risk control, and marginal certification, test language-mix shift, and
quantify the ``certification tax'' Hinglish users pay for a valid guarantee.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Routing between a cheap and a premium LLM is the standard way to cut inference cost
\citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm,ding2024hybrid,hu2024routerbench}.
Because router scores are miscalibrated, recent work replaces threshold tuning by
conformal calibration, giving a finite-sample bound on accuracy lost
\citep{dou2026conformalcascade,copRoute2026}. These guarantees are marginal. The
authors of Conformal Cascade note that ``the marginal guarantee gives no subgroup
protection'' \citep{dou2026conformalcascade}. For multilingual products, the subgroup that
matters is the user's language. Code-mixed users are common in India, are poorly served by
small models \citep{khanuja2020gluecos,indiromcom2026}, and already pay a tokenizer
premium \citep{petrov2023language}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) We show empirically that marginal certification of a cheap/premium router
mis-allocates risk between languages: at every $\alpha$ one group's violation rate exceeds
$\delta$ (paired GSM8K: English at 64.4\% / 50.1\% for $\alpha=0.05/0.10$; Hinglish at
53.3\% for $\alpha=0.20$), while the other group is over-protected.
(2) We certify per language (a Mondrian/stratified version of Learn-then-Test
\citep{vovk2003mondrian,angelopoulos2021ltt}). It keeps every group within budget, and the
guarantee survives test-time language-mix shift. (3) We measure what the guarantee
costs in savings (the certification tax) and compare against routers and calibration
rules from the literature. (4) We release a gateway that serves the certified per-language
router, and the pre-registration (\texttt{docs/research/PREREG.md}).

\section{Related work}
\textbf{Routing and cascades.} FrugalGPT \citep{chen2023frugalgpt}, AutoMix
\citep{aggarwal2023automix}, Hybrid LLM \citep{ding2024hybrid} and RouteLLM
\citep{ong2024routellm} learn when the cheap model suffices. RouterBench
\citep{hu2024routerbench} and LLMRouterBench \citep{llmrouterbench2026} standardise evaluation.
None of them gives a finite-sample guarantee or reports per-language risk.
\textbf{Certified routing.} Conformal Cascade \citep{dou2026conformalcascade} and CopRoute
\citep{copRoute2026} give distribution-free guarantees, and both are marginal.
\textbf{Stratified risk control.} Mondrian conformal prediction \citep{vovk2003mondrian}
and role-stratified CRC for tool calls \citep{rahman2026rolestratified} show that aggregate
control dilutes rare-group failures, a ``price of coarseness''. CS-WCP
\citep{cswcp2026} handles uncertain group proportions. We bring per-group certification to
LLM routing, with language as the stratum.
\textbf{Multilingual routing.} Cross-lingual success probes \citep{probes2026crosslingual}
calibrate routing signals across languages, but without code-mixing or finite-sample
guarantees.

\section{Method}
Let $u(x)=1$ if the cheap answer is wrong and the premium answer is right. A router with
score $s(x)$ and threshold $\lambda$ sends $x$ to the cheap model iff $s(x)\le\lambda$. Its
group risk is $R_g(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{s(X)\le\lambda\}u(X)\mid G=g]$, the
quality lost relative to always-premium. The scorer is a logistic regression on multilingual
MiniLM embeddings plus language flag, code-mix index and log length, trained on a
disjoint split. Thresholds are on a fixed grid of 101 training-score quantiles.

\paragraph{Marginal LTT.} One $\lambda$ is chosen by fixed-sequence testing of
$H_\lambda: R(\lambda)>\alpha$ with Clopper--Pearson p-values
$P[\mathrm{Bin}(n,\alpha)\le k]$ \citep{clopper1934} at level $\delta/|A|$.
\paragraph{Per-language LTT (ours).} One $\lambda_g$ per group at $\delta/(K|A|)$, $K=2$.
Then $P(R_g(\hat\lambda_g)>\alpha)\le\delta$ for every $g$ and every $\alpha\in A$,
including when the choice of $\alpha$ is made post hoc.
\paragraph{Why marginal fails.} If group $g$ has share $p_g$, a marginal guarantee at level
$\alpha$ allows $R_g$ up to $\alpha/p_g$. To protect $g$, a single threshold must certify at
$\alpha p_g$, which costs savings for every group
\citep[cf.][]{rahman2026rolestratified}.

\section{Experimental setup}
\textbf{Data.} GSM8K test items \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, each in English and a Hinglish
version generated by gpt-oss-20b, with automatic checks (numbers, script, language ID)
and one human check per item. Items are split by source item (seed 20261015) into
scorer\_train 30\%, pilot 10\% and cert 60\%. Both language versions stay in the same split.
Pair 2: cheap Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, premium gpt-oss-120b.
\emph{Main analysis:} 307 GSM8K cert items present in both languages (614 rows), so the two
groups ask identical problems. \emph{Secondary:} all 1{,}326 cert rows from 1{,}019 items,
adding English-only ARC-Challenge questions \src{grades\_pair2.jsonl}. Pair 1 (gpt-oss-20b $\to$ 120b) is
reported as underpowered.
\textbf{Protocol.} 1{,}000 item-grouped 50/50 calibration/test re-splits of cert,
$\delta=0.10$, $A=\{0.05,0.10,0.20\}$. Violation rate = share of re-splits with test
risk $>\alpha$; it should be $\le\delta$.
\textbf{Baselines.} Always-cheap, always-premium, random at matched cheap share;
uncalibrated empirical threshold (common practice in \citealp{ding2024hybrid,ong2024routellm});
conformal risk control \citep{angelopoulos2024crc}; marginal LTT (the marginal setting of
\citealp{dou2026conformalcascade}); and a $k$NN similarity-weighted scorer in the style of RouteLLM.
Conformal Cascade itself needs $N=16$ self-consistency samples per query. We do not have
these yet \pending{run on Kaggle with the local 3B model}.

\section{Results}
\textbf{The cheap model is worse for Hinglish.} On the same 307 problems the unrecoverable-error
rate is 11.7\% in English and 28.0\% in Hinglish; 71 problems fail only in Hinglish and 21 only
in English (exact McNemar $p=1.6\times10^{-7}$) \src{paired\_urate\_gsm8k.json}.

\textbf{Marginal certification mis-allocates risk.} At $\alpha=0.10$, marginal LTT violates
the guarantee for English in 50.1\% of re-splits while over-protecting Hinglish (1.5\%
violation, 14.9\% saving). Per-language LTT keeps both groups within budget
(Table~\ref{tab:main}) \src{router\_baselines\_table\_paired\_gsm8k.md}. On the larger
unpaired set the pattern is the same (marginal: English 43.3\% violation; per-language:
3.8\% / 2.3\%, saving 55.5\% / 22.1\%) \src{router\_baselines\_table.md}.

\textbf{Language-mix shift (H3).} With the test-time Hinglish share set to 10\% / 90\%,
marginal LTT violates in 38.5\% / 1.6\% of re-splits at $\alpha=0.10$ and in 0\% / 44.1\% at
$\alpha=0.20$, so it fails in whichever direction the mix moves. Per-language LTT stays
$\le 4.4\%$. \textbf{Scorer quality.} AUROC on cert: LR 0.635 (EN) / 0.579 (HI), $k$NN
0.523 / 0.545. The guarantee holds even with a weak scorer; scorer quality only moves the
savings. \textbf{Certification tax (H4).} The only single threshold that protects both
groups (marginal LTT at $\alpha/2$) gives English about the same saving as per-language
certification (57.5\% vs 53.2\%) but cuts Hinglish from 20.1\% to 1.3\%: under one shared
threshold, Hinglish users pay for the guarantee with almost all of their savings.
\textbf{Compression does not add savings under the guarantee.} Joint certified
compress-or-route never beats routing alone \src{KEY\_RESULTS F}.

\section{Limitations}
The main analysis is a single task (GSM8K math); the secondary set adds English-only ARC,
where language and task are confounded. Hinglish is machine-generated and then
human-checked, not naturally occurring. Two groups only, and 307 paired items (36 English
and 86 Hinglish positives), so savings at $\alpha=0.05$ are coarse.
Pair 1 (7 EN / 5 HI positives on cert) is too small to report. The cheap path in pair 2 is priced at
gpt-oss-20b list rates as a stand-in. The guarantee assumes exchangeability within each
language. Under shift it holds only if each group's own distribution is stable.

\section{Conclusion}
A router certified on average can be uncertified for the users who most need the premium
model. Certifying per language is cheap to implement and keeps the guarantee for every
group, at a measurable cost in savings.
